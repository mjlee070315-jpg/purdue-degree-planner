Smart Course Planner

Turning "when do I graduate?" into a constraint satisfaction problem.

Built around six real Purdue engineering curricula — Industrial, Mechanical, Electrical, Civil, and Chemical Engineering, plus Computer Science. Instead of manually checking prerequisites and credit caps every semester, it models each degree as a dependency graph, solves for the fastest feasible path to graduation, and proves — mathematically, not just empirically — how close that path is to optimal.

00 — Why I Built This
Two ordinary frustrations, one system.

I'm a First-Year Engineering student at Purdue, applying to transfer to another university, and this project came out of two very ordinary frustrations. First, just checking my own course plan meant logging into MyPurdue's DegreeWorks — every time, through a portal, with no quick way to just glance at where I stood. Second, and more specific to being in FYE: I had to choose a major without any real way to compare what each one actually required, or how the paths differed in length and structure. Industrial Engineering is what I'm most drawn to, so it's the major I built out first and in the most depth — the other five exist so I could compare paths side by side, not because I was undecided about IE itself.

Building it taught me two things I'd only had in the abstract before: how to take operations-research concepts I'd read about — critical path method, greedy scheduling, combinatorial relaxation bounds — and apply them to a problem that was actually mine, with real prerequisite data and real constraints. And how to take a genuinely messy set of requirements — six different majors, a live weekly timetable, a real academic calendar, GPA projection — and design them as one coherent system instead of six separate one-off scripts.

01 — The Problem
Every engineering student re-derives the same schedule by hand.

Purdue's First-Year Engineering program lets students explore majors before declaring — which is great for exploration, but it means the actual path from "undecided" to a specific degree is buried across catalog PDFs, prerequisite chains, and credit-cap rules that most students only discover by trial and error each advising cycle, and that path looks completely different depending which major you land on. The underlying question — "if I take X credits a semester, how many semesters until I graduate?" — is a scheduling problem with real structure: a dependency graph and a capacity constraint, repeated across six different degree structures. That structure is exactly what industrial engineers are trained to model.

02 — The Model
Framed as a scheduling optimization, not a checklist.

Each of the six degrees is represented as its own directed acyclic graph — 40 to 43 courses apiece. Solving "what should I take, and when" is a lightweight relative of an integer program:

Decision Variable
x[course, semester] ∈ {0, 1}

Whether a given course is taken in a given semester.

Constraints
prereqs(c) ⊆ completed-before(s)
Σ credits(s) ≤ cap

A course can't be scheduled until every prerequisite lands in a strictly earlier semester, and no semester exceeds the credit cap the student sets.

Objective
minimize Σ semesters

Reach graduation in as few semesters as possible without violating the constraints above.

The planner solves this with a greedy topological scheduler rather than a true ILP solver: at each step it computes the longest remaining dependency chain for every unfinished course (its distance from the capstone), and prioritizes courses on that critical path so they never become the bottleneck. Unlike a plain heuristic, though, the tool doesn't just trust the greedy result — it checks it. See the Complexity section below.

03 — Complexity & Optimality
Proving the schedule is good, not just hoping it is.

This scheduling problem — assign tasks with precedence constraints into the fewest possible capacity-limited batches — is a variant of precedence-constrained scheduling (related to P|prec|Cmax in scheduling-theory notation), which is NP-hard in general. For a graph with 40+ courses and a dozen-plus eligible electives per semester, brute-forcing every possible assignment isn't computationally realistic in a browser. Running an exact ILP solver (OR-Tools, CBC) would work, but at this scale a much cheaper technique does almost the same job:

Lower Bound 1 — Capacity
⌈ Σ remaining credits / cap ⌉

No schedule can finish faster than total remaining credits divided by the per-semester cap, rounded up — regardless of prerequisites.

Lower Bound 2 — Critical Path
longest chain + 1

If course E requires D requires C requires B requires A, that's 5 semesters minimum, no matter how much room is left in the credit cap.

The Check
greedy result == max(LB1, LB2) ?

If the greedy schedule matches the larger of the two bounds, it is provably optimal — no better schedule can exist, full stop.

This is a classic combinatorial relaxation bound: instead of solving the hard problem exactly, solve two easy relaxations of it and use whichever gives the stronger (larger) bound. Run against all six majors' full 8-semester plans at an 18-credit cap, the greedy scheduler matches this lower bound exactly for IE, ME, ChemE, and CE — provably optimal, zero semesters to spare. For ECE and CS it lands one semester above the bound — a small, honestly-reported gap, since closing it exactly would require exponential search. The planner shows this live: open any major and the optimizer panel displays the current gap (or the ✓ when it's zero).

The planner also runs a full Critical Path Method (CPM) analysis on each major — real forward/backward-pass Early Start, Early Finish, Late Start, Late Finish, and slack for every course, rendered as an interactive network diagram with the zero-slack critical path highlighted. CPM assumes unlimited parallel course-taking, so it's a genuinely different (and looser) relaxation than the credit-capped roadmap: comparing the two side by side — theoretical fastest path vs. realistic, resource-constrained path — is itself the kind of relaxation-vs-reality comparison operations research is built on.

04 — Features
What the planner actually does.

Six majors, one tool

Switch between IE, ME, ECE, CS, ChemE, and CE with one click — each with its own real course graph, progress tracking, and optimality check.

Set what you're taking now

Mark your actual current-semester courses (only shows what your completed prerequisites unlock) — the roadmap regenerates for everything after that.

Live re-scheduling

Drag the credit-cap slider and the entire remaining semester plan re-solves instantly — answers "how many semesters if I take 18 credits?" directly.

Progress tracking

Check off completed courses each semester. Progress is saved per-major locally, so switching majors never loses your place.

Optimality verification

Every schedule is checked against a combinatorial lower bound — you see whether the plan is provably optimal, not just "probably fine."

Graduation projection

Combines completed courses with the optimizer's remaining path to project an actual semester and year of graduation.

Actual + projected GPA

Enter actual grades for completed courses and predicted grades for courses in progress — see both your current GPA and where it's headed.

Timetable & calendar export

Build a weekly class-time grid with conflict detection, then export the whole semester — classes plus breaks and finals — as a real .ics calendar file.

05 — Data & Stack
Sourced from Purdue's own catalog and department handbooks.

Course lists, credit hours, and prerequisite chains come from Purdue's published plans of study and department curriculum handbooks for First-Year Engineering, Industrial, Mechanical, Electrical, Civil, and Chemical Engineering, and Computer Science. It's an unofficial planning aid, not a replacement for myPurduePlan / DegreeWorks or an academic advisor.
